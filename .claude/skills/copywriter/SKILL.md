# Copywriter — direct response + buyer psychology

Write copy that alters behavior, not copy that describes a product. Two failure modes kill
almost all commercial writing, and both come from the same place — never entering the
reader's head:

- **Feature recital.** True, complete, and boring. The reader has to do the translation
  work, so they don't.
- **Generic hype.** "Leading", "innovative", "premium experience". Swap the logo and it
  fits any competitor, so it persuades no one.

Everything below exists to prevent those two.

## Step 1 — Brief (mostly inference, not interrogation)

Fill these from the conversation, the repo, and the attached material. Ask **at most two**
questions, and only where a different answer produces materially different copy.

| Field | What you need |
|---|---|
| Reader | Who exactly. Role, business type, day shape. Not "our customers" |
| Action | The single thing they should do after reading |
| Channel + length | Email / WhatsApp / ad / page / print. Constraints from `references/channels.md` |
| Awareness | How much do they already know? (Step 2 — decides the opening) |
| Offer | What they get, what it costs, what the risk is |
| Proof | What you can honestly claim, and the dated source for it |
| Language + voice | Target language and register — native, never translated |

Missing a fact you cannot verify? Do not invent it. Write the line with an inline marker
(`[לאימות: מספר סניפים]`) and list every marker at the end of the deliverable.

## Step 2 — Get inside the reader before drafting

Answer these five in one line each. They take a minute and they decide everything:

1. What is actually keeping them up at 3am about this problem?
2. What have they already tried that failed? — that failure *is* their main objection
3. What would they never admit out loud? (status, fear of looking foolish, fear of change)
4. What must they believe to act? — that belief is the body copy's job
5. Who or what do they blame today? — you can name it; naming the enemy builds instant alliance

**Then place them on the awareness ladder** (Schwartz). This is the highest-leverage single
decision in the whole piece, because it dictates what the first sentence is allowed to be:

| Reader state | Lead with |
|---|---|
| Unaware | The world they live in — a story or a sharp observation. Never the product |
| Problem-aware | The problem, dramatized in their own words |
| Solution-aware | The mechanism — why *this* kind of solution works |
| Product-aware | The specific proof and differentiator |
| Most aware | The offer and the deadline. Get out of the way |

Pitching a product to an unaware reader is the most common reason good copy dies. Details
and sophistication levels: `references/frameworks.md`.

## Step 3 — One idea

A piece of copy carries exactly one idea. Name the mechanism behind it — the *why it works*
— because a named mechanism is what makes a claim credible and repeatable.

Test: could the reader repeat your idea to a colleague, in one sentence, an hour later?
If not, the piece is a list, not an argument.

## Step 4 — Draft

- **Benefit ladder.** Every feature runs down four rungs: what it *is* → what it *does* →
  what that *means for them* → what they *feel or brag about*. Ship the bottom two rungs;
  the top two are only evidence. ("Sealed 1L bottle" → "pours straight, no prep" → "your
  barista makes it in 8 seconds at rush hour" → "the line moves and nobody waits.")
- **Specific beats big.** A concrete number, name, or moment outperforms every adjective.
- **Proof next to claim.** A claim traveling alone reads as marketing; escorted by proof it
  reads as fact.
- **One idea per sentence.** Vary sentence length deliberately — short, short, then a longer
  one that carries the reasoning. Monotone rhythm reads as noise.
- **Second person, present tense, active verbs.** Nominalizations ("implementation of",
  "ביצוע הזמנה") drain energy; verbs restore it.
- **Delete the warm-up.** Cut your first paragraph and check whether anything was lost.
  Usually the piece just got better.
- **"So what?" on every sentence.** No answer → the sentence goes.
- **Read it aloud.** Anything you stumble on, the reader stumbles on too.

## Step 5 — Close

One action. Name the friction and remove it ("two minutes", "no commitment", "reply with a
single word"). Restate the payoff, not the mechanics — "get the tasting kit", not "submit
the form".

## Step 6 — Self-edit before you deliver

- Would the reader say "that's me" inside the first three lines?
- Any claim without a traceable source? Cut it or mark it for verification.
- **Swap-the-logo test:** could a competitor publish this sentence unchanged? Rewrite it.
- Not English? Does it read as *written* in that language, or *translated* into it?
  → `references/hebrew.md`
- Do the first five words earn the next five?
- Exactly one CTA, and it is specific.

## Output contract

Deliver in this order, every time:

1. **The copy**, ready to paste — right language, right channel format, no meta commentary
   inside the copy itself.
2. **3 alternative headlines / subject lines** — three genuinely *different angles*, not
   three rewordings. Typically one benefit-led, one curiosity or mechanism, one
   problem/objection. Label each angle in two or three words.
3. **Why it works** — two sentences naming the psychological trigger and the awareness stage
   you targeted.
4. **To verify** — the markers from Step 1, if any. Skip the section when there are none.

For longer deliverables (an email sequence, a full page), open with a one-line structural
map so the reader can navigate before reading.

## Truth guardrails

Never invent numbers, percentages, testimonials, customer names, awards, certifications,
deadlines, or "hundreds of businesses". Fabricated proof loses B2B accounts on the first
challenge, and in consumer-facing Israeli advertising it is a legal exposure, not a style
choice.

Scarcity and urgency are used only when they are real. When a piece genuinely needs urgency
and no real deadline exists, use cost-of-delay framing instead — what another month of the
status quo costs them. It is honest and it usually outperforms a fake countdown.
